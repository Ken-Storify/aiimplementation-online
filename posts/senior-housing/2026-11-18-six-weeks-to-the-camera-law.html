From the November 18, 2026 LITT Briefing

Six Weeks to the Camera Law

Picture January 2. A daughter walks in with a camera in a box and a consent form she printed off the internet. Her mother's roommate is asleep. The executive director has three questions to answer on the spot. Has the roommate consented? Which form is the right one? Who is allowed to watch the footage?

That moment is six weeks away. Illinois Public Act 104-0494 was signed on June 26, 2026. Starting January 1, 2027, it gives residents of assisted living and shared housing establishments the right to electronic monitoring in their own rooms (Illinois General Assembly). The law is not new. The calendar is.

The rules are specific. The resident pays for the device, the installation, the upkeep, the removal, and the internet service. The establishment may not charge for the electricity. Nobody on staff may access a recording without written consent from the resident or the resident's representative (ArentFox Schiff).

The roommate is where it gets hard. If a roommate refuses consent, the establishment has to make a reasonable attempt to accommodate, and if no room is open it has to reevaluate every two weeks. The burden of proving a requested accommodation is not reasonable sits with the establishment (Illinois General Assembly).

The stakes are real. Retaliating against a resident over monitoring is a business offense with a fine of up to $10,000. A recording that has not been edited and carries its date and time can be admitted in civil, criminal, and administrative proceedings (Hinshaw & Culbertson).

Now stand where the caregiver stands. Picture her finishing a transfer she has done a thousand times, exactly the way she was trained to do it. Thirty seconds of that, with no context, looks rough. It was not. That is what "they don't trust us" feels like on the floor. She deserves a straight answer before the first box arrives: what the law allows, what it does not, and that the community will not watch resident footage without written consent.

Treat it as a consent workflow, not a technology rollout. And you are not the one choosing the device.

That matters more as operators install AI monitoring of their own. On November 4, SafelyYou announced that three more Senior Lifestyle memory care communities had gone live on its Halo platform, which pairs fall detection with virtual check-ins (SafelyYou via Business Wire). One of the three, North Shore Place, is in Northbrook, Illinois. A system the operator chooses and a camera the resident installs are two different things, with two different consent paths.

One detail to get right before you order signs. Nursing homes have to post a notice at every building entrance. Assisted living establishments are exempt from that one. What they do need is a sign at the entrance to each monitored room. The wording is set by statute: "This room is electronically monitored." The establishment installs it and maintains it.

By December 15, an Illinois operator should be able to put five things on the table. A written intake process built on the consent form the Illinois Department of Public Health prescribes. A roommate-accommodation script, with the two-week reevaluation on a calendar. A stock of room signs and a named person who posts them. A one-page explainer for staff. A plain statement that the community does not view resident recordings without written consent.

Operators outside Illinois can run the same drill now. This is the kind of law that travels.
