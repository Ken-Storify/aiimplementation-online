From the November 11, 2026 LITT Briefing

Delaware Drafted the Unelected Officer

Picture the next board packet. Between the quarterly financials and the compliance update, there's a line about an AI vendor renewal, framed as routine. Nobody on that board thinks they're making a governance decision. They're approving a tool. What that tool is allowed to do gets decided later, by someone who wasn't in the room.

Delaware is trying to settle that question in law. The state is drafting a new entity, the Artificial Intelligence Company, that an AI agent would run day to day. It could sign contracts, make decisions, and sue or be sued in the company's name. Its backers would be shielded from liability unless they underfunded it or used it for fraud or a willful violation of law (Insurance Business).

On October 26, House Oversight Chair James Comer announced an investigation and set a November 9 deadline for records. The governor's office said it was "in the process of reviewing" the letter (Bloomberg Law), and as of this writing nothing newer is on the record.

A legislator on the drafting subcommittee describes the guardrails: no business with the general public during the sandbox, required disclosure to anyone the company transacts with, and action logs reviewed by an attorney. She also says the state's AI Commission still has questions, and the draft hasn't gone to the legislature (Delaware Public Media).

The quieter version of this story is already playing out inside ordinary companies. Nobody puts an AI agent in charge on purpose. Authority builds up anyway, one deployment at a time, until the tool is acting like an officer nobody elected. I call it the Unelected Officer. Delaware is trying to write it down.

The same report says the subcommittee named healthcare among the sectors it hopes to admit to the sandbox. Healthcare leaders who assume this is a tech-company story should read that line twice.

The real question isn't about Delaware. It's who decided. Every company is answering it right now, either on purpose or by default. That's what governance theater costs. Leaders care. The decisions just get made for them faster than their boards can notice.

This month, run one inventory. List every AI tool in production, and next to each one, write how far it can go before a named person has to approve it: sign, pay, send something public in the company's name. Once that list exists, the board conversation stops being abstract. It becomes a set of yes-or-no decisions about specific tools.
