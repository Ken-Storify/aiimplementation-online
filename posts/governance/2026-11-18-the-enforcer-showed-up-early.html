From the November 18, 2026 LITT Briefing

The Enforcer Showed Up Early

Usually a law takes effect and enforcement shows up a year later. New York flipped the order.

On November 17, Attorney General Letitia James created the AI Safety, Standards, Enforcement, and Transparency (ASSET) Unit to enforce the RAISE Act, which takes effect January 1, 2027 and requires covered AI developers to disclose safety measures and report incidents (New York Attorney General). The enforcer exists six weeks before there is anything to enforce.

The next day, California released recommendations under Governor Newsom's AI executive order: mandatory independent testing, expanded incident reporting, and state emergency shutdown authority over frontier models (Office of the Governor). These are recommendations, not law. They close the loop on the kill-switch study I covered on September 23.

If you buy AI tools rather than build them, the first reaction is relief. These rules aim at frontier developers, not you. That relief is half right.

Read it as your vendor's obligation, not someone else's law, and your vendor's obligation is your leverage. The models those developers build already sit inside the tools your staff use. And the ASSET Unit also holds broad civil authority over unfair and deceptive practices, plus the SHIELD Act, which reaches far beyond AI labs (New York Attorney General).

Rob Reich, one of the experts behind the California report, put the gap bluntly: "Today there is more oversight of the cafeteria offering sandwiches to employees at a frontier AI developer than of the AI models they design" (Office of the Governor).

This month, list every AI tool you pay for and ask each vendor two questions in writing. What safety framework does your underlying model provider publish? Will you notify us if a safety incident affects our product? Log the answers. A vendor who cannot answer is your first red flag.
